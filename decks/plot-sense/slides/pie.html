08 · Pie charts

Pies hide close differences

A pie asks you to compare angles. With five shares between 16% and 24%, that is close to guessing.

Sorted bars put the same numbers on one baseline, so rank and size read instantly.

Part-to-whole with many parts? One stacked bar keeps the whole visible and still uses length.

Eye rule: pie only for 2–3 parts

Or when one part dominates and the message is “most of the whole”. Otherwise: sorted bars.

Plot Sense · 12

A pie shows parts of a whole, and that is its one strength: everyone sees immediately that the slices add up to 100 percent. Its weakness is that it encodes value as angle and area, which people judge poorly. The animation shows three sets of five shares, each between 16 and 24 percent and summing to 100. In the pie, picking the largest slice is hard and ranking all five is harder. In the sorted bar chart beside it the same numbers line up on one baseline and the order is obvious. Use a pie for two or three parts, or when one part is clearly dominant and the message is about the whole. For anything that needs ranking or close comparison, use sorted bars, or a single stacked bar if part-to-whole matters.
