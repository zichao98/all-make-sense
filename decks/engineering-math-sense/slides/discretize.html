03 · Discretize

Sample too slowly and you see a fake signal

To capture frequency f, take more than two samples per cycle (Nyquist).

The trap: a 9 Hz wave sampled 10 times a second gives exactly the samples of a 1 Hz wave. The data cannot tell them apart.

Same idea elsewhere: pixels per edge in an image, time steps per oscillation in a simulation.

Eye rule: two samples per wiggle is the floor

Below it, fast detail does not disappear. It folds into slow detail that looks real.

Engineering Math Sense · 06

Discretization is the first place information can be lost without any warning. The sampling theorem says that to represent a frequency f you need a sampling rate above 2f. The animation samples at 10 Hz. At 2 Hz and 4 Hz there are more than two samples per cycle and the orange curve through the samples is the true wave. At 6 Hz and 9 Hz there are fewer. The samples of a 9 Hz sine at 10 Hz are exactly the samples of a 1 Hz sine with the sign flipped, and 6 Hz looks like 4 Hz. This is aliasing. The same logic applies to images, where an edge needs enough pixels, and to simulations, where a time step that is too large for the fastest oscillation produces nonsense. Real systems sample with margin above the theoretical floor.
